How a tankless heater works and what it asks of the house

A tankless water heater has no storage tank. When a hot tap opens, a flow sensor tells the unit to fire, and water passes through a heat exchanger and leaves hot. A gas model uses a burner and a fan-driven vent, and an electric model uses large heating elements with very high amperage.

That design changes what the house must supply. A gas unit often needs a larger gas line and special stainless or polypropylene vent pipe. An electric unit may need several 240-volt circuits, which an older panel cannot always supply.

Tankless heaters are rated by gallons per minute at a stated temperature rise, not by tank size. ENERGY STAR defines the figure as the gallons per minute the unit can supply while holding its nominal temperature, measured over a 67 degree rise.

Tankless against a tank, without the sales pitch

Neither choice is right for every house, and a tankless unit is not always the smarter buy.

A conventional tank

  • Lower equipment and installation work in most homes
  • Reuses existing gas, vent and electrical connections
  • Runs out when the stored volume is used
  • Wears from the inside as sediment and rust build

A tankless unit

  • Hot water does not run out while the flow stays within its rating
  • Takes less floor space and can mount on a wall
  • Often needs a new vent, gas line or electrical circuit
  • Needs periodic flushing to clear scale from the heat exchanger
  • Short taps and very low flows can give a brief cold gap

Sizing a unit by flow and temperature rise

Tankless sizing starts with how many fixtures run at once and how much the incoming water has to be warmed. A shower, a kitchen faucet and a dishwasher together can add up to a flow that a small unit cannot keep hot.

Georgia's state minimum plumbing code says tankless heaters are sized and installed per the manufacturer's recommendations. That puts the sizing table inside the product's installation manual, not the plumbing code, so the installer works from the manual for the unit you pick.

Incoming water is colder in winter

The unit has to lift a fixed temperature, and the colder the supply, the lower the flow it can sustain at the same setting. A unit that feels generous in September may feel marginal in January.

A big household needs a plan, not a bigger label

A 100 gallon tank has no tankless equivalent, since tankless units are not sized in gallons. Households with several bathrooms may use two units or a larger model, and the installer should show you the flow math.

The expansion tank question

Georgia's amended plumbing code calls for a thermal expansion control device on a STORAGE water heater supplied through a check valve, pressure reducing valve or backflow preventer. The text is about storage heaters. It does not make that requirement for tankless units, and a tankless installation follows the manufacturer's manual and your plumber's judgment.

Gwinnett County Water Resources says the pressure reducing valve belongs to the homeowner, so a pressure test at an outdoor spigot is worth doing before any new heater goes in.

When a tankless unit needs repair

Most callers notice a pattern before an error code. The panel on the unit also flashes a code that narrows down the cause.

Cold water between hot bursts

Water that goes lukewarm or cold mid-shower can mean a clogged inlet screen, scale on the heat exchanger or a flow sensor that is not reading.

Error code or no ignition

A gas unit may fail to light from low gas pressure, a blocked vent or a dirty flame rod. Check the manual for the code, then call.

Water leaking from the unit

A tankless heater that has started to leak from the cabinet needs the water and power off right away, and a plumber to find the failed fitting or heat exchanger.

Reduced flow from every tap

The inlet filter may be plugged, or scale inside the heat exchanger may be narrowing the passage.

Popping or knocking sounds

Scale breaking off the exchanger or air in the line often causes it. A flush usually settles it.

Descaling flush, in the order it is done

Mineral scale and sediment coat the inside of the heat exchanger over time and cut flow and efficiency. Gwinnett County Water Resources reports very soft water, with hardness of roughly 1.05 to 1.87 grains per gallon, so Suwanee units scale slowly. A yearly flush is still the common manufacturer recommendation.

  1. Power down and isolate

    Unit power is switched off and the gas valve closed on a gas model. The hot and cold isolation valves are closed.

  2. Hook up the flush kit

    Two hoses attach to the service ports, one running to a small pump and a bucket of descaling solution, one returning to the bucket.

  3. Circulate the solution

    The pump moves the solution through the heat exchanger for about an hour, long enough to loosen scale.

  4. Rinse and clean the screen

    Clean water goes through to clear the solution, and the inlet filter screen is cleaned or replaced.

  5. Restore and test

    Valves are reopened, power and gas return, and the unit is fired and checked for steady output.

Gas supply, venting and why they matter

Gas-fired water heaters and their venting fall under the fuel gas code. A tankless gas unit burns a lot of gas at once, so the gas line must carry the full load. If the line is undersized, the unit may not hold temperature or may shut down under a heavy demand.

If you smell gas near the heater, leave the building and call the gas utility, for most homes Atlanta Gas Light at 877.427.4321, or 911, before calling a plumber. Do not try to find the leak yourself, and avoid switches and flames.

The City of Suwanee building permits page lists water heater replacement among work needing a permit, with the inspections line at 770-945-8996. Ask the installer how the permit and inspection will be handled for a tankless conversion, since new gas or vent work may be involved.

What decides a tankless price

Equipment is only part of it. The work around the unit often matters more, and you hear the clear price before work starts.

Unit capacity

Larger flow ratings and condensing models cost more.

Gas line and vent

A new gas run or sealed vent pipe through the wall or roof can be the biggest line item.

Electrical work

Electric models may need new breakers, larger wire or a panel upgrade.

Condensate and drain

Condensing gas units need a drain for acidic condensate.

Maintenance plan

Yearly flushing and filter cleaning are an ongoing cost that a tank does not have.

Home insurance usually covers sudden accidental leaks, not wear, scale damage or planned upgrades. Check the policy before assuming a failed heat exchanger is covered.
