Plumbing Vent Problems in Suwanee, GA

A plumbing vent is the pipe that lets air into your drain lines, and when it clogs, drains gurgle and flow slows. Here is how to tell a vent problem from a clog and what is safe to check yourself.

What the vent does and how it gets blocked

A plumbing vent is a pipe that runs from your drain lines up through the roof. It lets air in behind moving water, so drains empty smoothly and the water seal in each trap stays put. A blocked vent starves the system of air, and the first signs are gurgling, slow drains and a sewer smell.

The usual culprits are a bird or wasp nest, leaves and debris dropped into the open pipe, a cracked or crushed section inside a wall, or a vent that was cut off or capped during a remodel. Most vent trouble starts at the roof end, where the pipe is exposed to everything that falls on it.

Gurgling, slow drains and smells: the vent clues

One slow sink usually means a local clog. A vent problem tends to show up in more than one place, or in sounds rather than standing water.

Gurgling after a flush

Air that cannot come in from the roof gets pulled through the nearest trap instead, so a tub or sink burbles when the toilet flushes.

Slow drains across the house

Water drags in a pipe when no air follows it, much like a bottle held upside down with the cap on.

A sewer smell in the bathroom

If the vacuum pulls a trap dry, sewer gas comes up through the drain. The smell is stronger after a big flush.

A toilet that flushes weakly or glugs

A bowl that takes a long time to settle and glugs while it does is a classic vent clue, as long as the main line is clear.

Checks you can make from the ground

None of these needs a ladder. Run them in order and note what changes.

  1. Test one fixture at a time

    Run the sink, then the tub, then flush the toilet. Write down which ones gurgle and which are slow.

  2. Listen at a tub or shower drain

    If a toilet flush makes the tub drain burp, air is being pulled from the wrong place.

  3. Look at the roof from the yard

    Walk around the house and find the vent pipes, usually a plain round pipe poking through the shingles. Binoculars help you spot a nest or a missing cap.

  4. Check the main line cleanout

    If water also rises in a floor drain, the problem may be a clogged main line and not a vent. That is a different repair.

Why a drain needs air behind the water

A 1.28 gallon flush leaves the bowl quickly and pushes air ahead of it down a drain that is often 3 inches wide. Without an open vent, water leaving the pipe creates suction behind it. That suction pulls on the nearest trap, which is the bend of standing water that blocks sewer gas.

A trap holds only a few inches of water. It does not take much suction to pull one down, which is why a vent problem often shows up as smells before it shows up as a backup.

Suwanee note: the 6-inch vent rule and the city permit

Georgia's state minimum plumbing code is the International Plumbing Code with Georgia amendments, adopted by the Department of Community Affairs. One amendment in the DCA packet says open vent pipes that extend through a roof shall be terminated not less than 6 inches above the roof. If your vent looks very short on a low-slope roof or after a re-roof, ask a plumber to check the height.

That one line is the only vent rule we cite. Venting also has rules about pipe size, slope and distance that are not covered here, and they depend on the code edition in force for your job. Ask your installer which rules apply to your house instead of relying on a number you read online.

The City of Suwanee permit page says changes to plumbing systems need a building permit, and it lists 770-945-8996 for permits and inspections. Adding a vent line, moving a fixture or re-routing a stack counts as a plumbing change, so call the city before work starts.

What a homeowner can do and what belongs to a plumber

Keep your feet on the ground unless you are comfortable on a roof. A vent opening is usually the highest, most exposed point of the house.

Safe for you to do

  • Find the vent pipes from the yard and photograph the tops.
  • Note which fixtures gurgle, and when.
  • Check the attic or a basement wall for a vent pipe that has been capped or cut off.

Better left to a plumber

  • Climbing onto the roof to clear or cap a vent.
  • Running cable or a camera down the vent line.
  • Repairing a cracked pipe, flashing or boot around the vent.
  • Adding an air admittance valve or a new vent line.

Roof work is where people get hurt

A roof can be wet, steep or slick with pollen, and a fall from even a single story causes serious injury. Do not climb on a wet roof, do not climb alone, and stay clear of power lines at the service drop. If a vent is out of reach from a ladder, call someone with the right fall protection.

If the smell inside the house is like rotten eggs, do not assume it is sewer gas. Natural gas smells like rotten eggs too. Leave the building, then call the gas utility, for most homes Atlanta Gas Light at 877.427.4321, or 911, before you call a plumber.

What a plumber actually does about a blocked vent

The first job is to separate a vent problem from a drain clog. A plumber runs water in several fixtures and listens, then looks at the roof end of the pipe. If the line is blocked at the top, the fix may be as simple as clearing the nest and fitting a cap suited to the pipe.

A cable or a small camera can be fed down the vent from the roof to find a blockage inside the wall. A cracked or crushed section is a repair, not a cleaning, and may mean opening the wall. If the camera shows the vent clear, the cause is probably a clogged main line, and sewer line cleaning or drain cleaning is the next step.

Some older additions and remodels use an air admittance valve, a one-way valve that lets air in without a roof pipe. Whether one is acceptable for your job is a code question, so ask the plumber and the city. Homeowners in Duluth and Lawrenceville face the same questions when a remodel adds a bathroom.

The International Plumbing Code with Georgia amendments is the state minimum standard that covers drain, waste and vent systems. According to the City of Suwanee's building permits page, changes to plumbing need a city permit.
