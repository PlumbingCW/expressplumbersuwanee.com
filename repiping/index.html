What a repipe actually replaces

A repipe replaces the pressurized water supply lines in a house, the pipes that carry water from the main shutoff to every sink, shower, toilet and appliance. It usually leaves the drain, waste and vent pipes alone, because those fail for different reasons and are a separate job.

Some repipes cover the whole house. Others replace one stretch, such as the line feeding a bathroom group, the run through a crawl space, or a single failing section tied back into good pipe.

The pipe from the street meter to the house is a different question. Gwinnett County Water Resources says everything on the customer side of the meter is the homeowner's responsibility, so a repipe can include that yard line when it is the weak point.

Signs the pipes, not the fixtures, are the problem

One leaky faucet is a fixture problem. A pattern across the house points at the pipe.

Repeated leaks in different places

A pinhole in the kitchen this year and another in the hall bath next year often means the metal is thinning all along the line, so patching one spot only waits for the next.

Brown or rust-colored water at the tap

Rust in the water when you first open a faucet points to corroding steel or iron pipe somewhere in the supply.

Low flow at every fixture

Galvanized steel can narrow from the inside as rust builds up, so showers weaken even though the meter and the pressure reducing valve test fine.

Stains, bubbling paint or damp drywall

A wall that stays damp or a ceiling stain that returns after drying can mean a slow leak on a joint you cannot see.

A known problem pipe material

If a plumber has identified polybutylene or aging galvanized in your walls, replacement is a planning question, not a wait-and-see one.

PEX, copper, CPVC and the rest, side by side

There is no single best pipe. The choice depends on the house, the water, and how much wall has to be opened.

MaterialWhere it does wellHonest drawback
PEXFlexible tubing that snakes through framing with fewer joints, which means less wall opened upNot for sunlight or outdoor exposure, and it needs the right fitting system
CopperRigid, long used in homes, and fine in hot water lines and exposed runsMore joints to solder, more labor, and a material cost that moves with the metal market
CPVCRigid plastic that is easy to cut and glue, often chosen for budget jobsCan turn brittle with age and heat, and it is less forgiving when bumped
Galvanized steelAlready in many older houses, so it is what you may be replacingCorrodes from the inside and is not what anyone should choose for new work

Ask the installer why they recommend one material for your house, and which fitting system they use.

Whole house or just one section

A partial repipe makes sense when one branch has failed and the rest tests sound. Replacing the bathroom group supply lines, for example, can cure repeated leaks without opening every wall.

The catch is the tie-in. Joining new pipe to old pipe of a different metal needs the right transition fitting, and the old pipe next to the joint may fail next if it is the same age as the section you replaced.

When partial is the better call

Leak history is limited to one area, the rest of the pipe is a modern material, and water pressure and color are normal elsewhere.

When whole-house wins

Several leaks in different rooms, rust in the water at every tap, or a material known to age out. Doing it once means less disruption than three smaller jobs spread over several years.

What open-wall work looks like in your house

Repiping is not invisible. Pipes live inside walls, floors and ceilings, so the work means access holes.

  1. Walk-through and routing

    The plumber looks at the attic, crawl space or basement first. Pipes are routed through those open areas wherever they can, to keep wall cuts to a minimum.

  2. Water shut off and drained

    The supply is closed at the main and the old lines are drained. Plan on stretches of the day without water and ask how long the stretches will be for your house.

  3. Access cuts in drywall

    Small openings are cut at fixtures and corners so new pipe can be fed through. Ask who handles patching and painting, which is often a separate trade.

  4. New pipe, pressure test, restore

    New lines are connected, filled and pressure tested for leaks before the walls are closed. The city may inspect the work afterward.

Suwanee housing age and what it means for your pipes

The city's own housing plan says 60 percent of Suwanee's housing units date from 2000 or later, and that the count jumped from 825 units in 1990 to 3,144 by the end of that decade. That tells you about home age, not about which pipe is inside your walls.

So check your own house. A home from the 1990s boom is now past thirty years old. Look at the pipe where it is exposed, near the water heater or under a sink, or ask a plumber to identify it.

Gwinnett County Water Resources reports very soft water at 1.05 to 1.87 grains per gallon, with an average of 1.34. Soft water leaves little mineral scale, so if flow has dropped, the likelier suspects are pipe corrosion or a failing pressure reducing valve, which is the homeowner's to maintain.

What moves the price of a repipe

Size and stories

More fixtures and more floors mean more pipe, more fittings and more routing.

Pipe material

PEX, copper and CPVC differ in material and labor, and copper moves with the metal market.

Access

An open crawl space is simple. Finished walls, tile and tight attics add time.

Whole house or partial

Replacing one branch costs less than the full house, but see the tie-in issue above.

Patching and finish work

Drywall, paint and tile repair may be quoted separately or left to you, so ask.

Home insurance generally covers sudden accidental water damage, not the cost of replacing worn pipe. Read the policy, and ask your agent whether a repiped system changes your coverage or premium.
