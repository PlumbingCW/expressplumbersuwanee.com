What a sewer camera actually shows

A sewer inspection is a recorded camera pass through the private line that runs from your house to the sewer connection. The camera sits on a flexible push rod, with a light and a transmitter in the head, and it enters through a cleanout.

You see the pipe material, the joints, any cracks or offset sections, roots, grease, a sag where water pools and the end of the line where it meets the connection. The footage is marked by distance, so a problem 62 feet in can be found again from above.

Inspection is information only. It does not fix anything, and it is useful because it tells you whether the next step is nothing, a cleaning or a repair.

Reasons people book a camera pass

None of these are emergencies by themselves, which is the point. A planned look costs less than an unplanned failure.

Buying or selling a home

A line that looks fine from the toilet may have root intrusion or a belly, and the footage informs the offer.

A line that has been cleared twice

Repeat clogs have a cause, and the camera finds it instead of paying for a third cable run.

Slow drains with no clear fixture

When several fixtures lag, a look at the shared line saves guessing.

Big trees near the lateral

Roots reach sewer joints, and a camera shows how far they have come before a backup does.

Before a driveway or patio project

Knowing the line path and condition helps avoid cutting or crushing it.

How the appointment goes

A camera visit is quiet and fairly quick, and you can watch.

  1. Find the access

    The technician locates the cleanout, usually outside near the foundation, and opens it.

  2. Clear what blocks the view

    If grease or debris cloud the lens, a light cleaning pass comes first and is explained before it happens.

  3. Run the camera

    The head moves toward the sewer connection, and the footage is watched live and recorded.

  4. Mark the trouble spots

    A locator can trace the head from the surface, so depth and position of any defect are marked.

  5. Review the footage together

    Ask for a plain description of what was seen and for a copy of the video.

Who owns the pipe and what the county says about checking it

Gwinnett County Water Resources says the property owner is responsible for the private sewer lateral, which runs to the edge of the right-of-way and includes the connection to the public sewer. Its sewer lateral page suggests having the lateral inspected by a licensed plumber, and says buyers should consider adding a lateral inspection to the home inspection.

Gwinnett County standards require a cleanout on the lateral at the right-of-way, and many homes also have one closer to the house. A camera usually goes in through the nearer one, so knowing where it is saves time.

The same county page notes that Georgia plumbing code requires a sanitary backwater valve where the lowest fixture sits below the next upstream manhole. A backwater valve is the homeowner's to maintain, and a camera visit is a good time to ask whether you have one.

Most Suwanee homes are on county sewer, but the city's plans note that some older areas still use septic. Tell us up front if you are not sure, because a septic system has no sewer lateral to scope.

Reading a bellied pipe

A sewer line needs about a quarter inch of fall per foot to keep waste moving. On camera, a belly shows up as a stretch of standing water in the pipe after the flow has passed. A small belly may just collect some paper over time, while a deep one points to cleaning more often or repair.

Getting the most from your inspection

A few minutes of preparation makes the footage more useful.

  • Find the cleanout cap and clear the area around it.
  • Write down which drains are slow and when the trouble started.
  • Mention any earlier clogs, roots or work on the line.
  • Ask whether the camera can reach the sewer connection.
  • Request a copy of the footage and notes for your file.
