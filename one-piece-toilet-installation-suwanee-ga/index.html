One-Piece Toilet Installation in Suwanee, GA

A one-piece toilet swap takes a few hours and a second person, because the fixture is a single heavy piece of porcelain. The work goes wrong at the flange, the wax ring and the bolts, not at the tank.

What installing a one-piece toilet actually involves

A one-piece toilet has the tank and bowl molded as a single unit, so there is no tank-to-bowl gasket to leak and no seam to clean. The tradeoff is weight and a lower margin for error. You cannot carry the bowl and tank separately, and you cannot set the fixture down on the flange to adjust it.

The job is simple to describe. Shut the water, remove the old toilet, check the flange, set a new wax ring, lower the toilet straight down onto the bolts, tighten, connect the supply and test. Most failures come from skipping the check of the flange or from overtightening a nut on glazed porcelain.

What to have on the floor before you start

Laying out everything first keeps you from leaving an open drain pipe uncovered while you drive to the store.

  • New wax ring or wax-free seal sized for your flange
  • Closet bolts and plastic caps (new ones, the old brass bolts are often corroded)
  • New braided supply line, since the old one can be rigid and cracked
  • Adjustable wrench, putty knife, level and a rag or old towels
  • A rag or wooden plug to stop sewer gas from the open pipe
  • A helper who can lift the toilet with you

Measure from the wall behind the toilet to the center of the closet bolts. A standard rough-in is 12 inches, and a toilet built for a different rough-in will not sit right.

Removing the old toilet and setting the new one

Work in this order. Each step protects the next.

  1. Shut off and drain

    Close the supply valve, flush, and sponge the last water out of the tank and bowl so it does not slosh when you lift.

  2. Disconnect and unbolt

    Unscrew the supply line from the tank, pop the caps off the closet bolts and remove the nuts. A seized nut can be cut with a hacksaw blade.

  3. Rock and lift

    Rock the toilet gently to break the wax seal, then lift straight up with a helper. Set it on towels, not directly on finished flooring.

  4. Scrape and inspect

    Remove old wax from the flange with a putty knife. The flange should sit flat on, or slightly above, the finished floor and be solid, not cracked or rusted.

  5. Seat the new toilet

    Place the wax ring on the flange or on the toilet horn, drop the toilet straight onto the bolts without twisting it, and press down evenly.

  6. Tighten, connect, test

    Alternate between nuts, a little at a time, until the base stops rocking. Reconnect the supply, open the valve, flush several times and check the base for any seep.

The flange decides whether the seal holds

A toilet seals at the wax ring pressed between the toilet horn and the closet flange. If the flange sits below the finished floor, because new tile was laid over it, the ring may not compress enough to seal and the base can leak at the floor. Repair rings and flange extenders exist for that case.

A bowl that rocks even after tightening usually means the floor is not flat, not that the nuts are loose. Plastic shims under the base fix a small gap. Cranking the nuts to close a big gap is how porcelain cracks.

Suwanee note: the 1.28 gallon rule and the city permit page

Georgia's amended plumbing code, published by the Department of Community Affairs, defines a high efficiency toilet as one whose average flush volume does not exceed 1.28 gallons. The state code requires high efficiency fixtures in all new construction. A replacement in an existing home is not a new build, so check the box label: a 1.28 gallon per flush toilet meets the benchmark either way.

The EPA WaterSense page on residential toilets says labeled models use 1.28 gallons per flush or less while still providing equal or superior performance, and that the average family can reduce water used for toilets by 20 to 60 percent. In practice, look for the WaterSense label on the box and on the tank, and keep the paperwork.

The City of Suwanee building permit page says permits are required for changes to plumbing systems. It does not spell out whether a like-for-like toilet swap counts, so call the city inspections line at 770-945-8996 before you start, and ask your installer to confirm the answer for your address.

Install it yourself or hire the job out

A straight swap on a sound floor is a reasonable weekend job. Problems under the floor are not.

Doing it yourself fits when

  • The old toilet came out cleanly and the flange is solid.
  • The floor is flat and dry with no soft spots.
  • You have a helper to lift the fixture.
  • You are replacing the same rough-in size.

A plumber fits when

  • The flange is cracked, rusted or below the finished floor.
  • The subfloor is soft or stained around the base.
  • The drain line is slow or the toilet has clogged repeatedly.
  • You are moving the toilet or adding a new bathroom.

One-piece, two-piece and wall-hung compared

The type you choose changes how hard the install is and what can fail later.

TypeInstall effortWatch for
One-pieceHeavy, no tank bolts to alignNeeds a flat floor and careful lowering
Two-pieceLighter pieces, easier to carryTank-to-bowl gasket and bolts can leak
Wall-hungNeeds an in-wall carrier and framingPlumber job, not a swap

Any type sits on the same closet flange, except wall-hung, which uses a carrier.

Signs the flange or drain is the real problem

If the floor around the old toilet was stained, spongy or smelled of sewage, the leak was at the wax ring for a while. The subfloor may need repair before a new fixture goes on.

A toilet that backs up after a clean install is a drain question, not a toilet question. A main-line clog can show up first at the lowest fixture, which is where drain cleaning or a sewer line cleaning visit comes in.
